Abstract: Document-parsing metrics can give high scores to outputs that change important facts and penalize changes that preserve meaning. People can often judge these consequences more easily than they can design a metric to measure them. We introduce a method for developing parsing metrics from severity policies that specify how each error type should affect the score. We express these policies through test scenarios that specify how a metric should behave. We then optimize the metric implementations against these fixed expectations, using coding agents to refine comparison rules, error detectors and weights under human review. This makes stated human priorities an explicit objective of metric design. We apply this method in ParseBench-2, a comprehensive document-parsing benchmark that combines broad capability coverage with metrics calibrated to error severity. It covers 4,317 pages across six dimensions of parsing quality: text, formatting, tables, charts, visual grounding, and forms. We use detailed annotations and targeted difficult cases to check for changed facts, broken structural relationships, and missing content. We take the lower of a parsing score that measures content recovery and a severity cap that penalizes detected errors, so recovering most of a page cannot hide a serious error. Across six severity test suites, our metrics more closely match the expert judgments expressed by the scenario targets than existing metrics and penalize at most 3.4% of the tested benign variations. We conduct a comprehensive evaluation spanning frontier vision-language models, specialized document parsers and open-weight systems, comparing their overall rankings, performance across parsing capabilities and tradeoffs between accuracy and cost.

Abstract: Contrastive vision-language models such as CLIP are trained to align images and text, not to represent taxonomies. Yet empirical studies show that CLIP embeddings exhibit hierarchical structure aligned with the hierarchy of semantic concepts. A theoretical characterization of why such hierarchy emerges from a non-hierarchical objective remains open. In this paper, we formalize this phenomenon as Hierarchical Angular Separation (HAS), a geometric property whereby each non-leaf concept embedding is more similar to its descendants than to off-branch concepts. Empirically, we show that HAS holds approximately in raw images and randomly initialized CLIP image embeddings, while in text embeddings it holds approximately only after contrastive learning. The key mechanism is the hierarchical co-occurrence structure of image-text pairs, which biases text embeddings toward root-centered image prototypes. For a linear contrastive loss, we derive a closed-form solution that makes this mechanism explicit. We extend the analysis to the InfoNCE loss and establish analogous guarantees under sufficient regularization. Synthetic experiments validate the theory and show that HAS is transferred even beyond the regimes covered by our analysis.

Abstract: Pixel losses train image reconstruction networks toward a point statistic of the target distribution, often averaging away fine textures under uncertainty. Perceptual losses alleviate this issue using pretrained features, but those features are inherently domain-specific. We study a random filter loss (RaFL) that compares prediction and target through filters with random weights, avoiding both pretrained features and task-specific loss engineering. RaFL combines random convolutions, random Fourier features, and random wavelet filters, automatically balancing their contributions through gradient-norm matching, eliminating manual loss-weight tuning and grid search. We systematically investigate three design choices: filter family, weight resampling schedule, and weight initialization. These findings suggest that RaFL is a simple, tuning-free, and domain-agnostic loss objective for 2D and 3D image reconstruction.
