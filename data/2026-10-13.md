Abstract: Autoregressive video diffusion models maintain a growing attention key-value (KV) cache, making memory a key bottleneck for long video generation. Recently, clustering-based quantization has emerged as an effective method to compress video KV caches by grouping similar key and value vectors then quantizing residual errors. However, when later frames attend to the compressed history, small reconstruction errors can compound as the video grows. Beyond the within-layer similarity exploited by clustering, we find strong dependencies across cache layers in video models. This motivates Coral-KV - Calibrated Offline, Residual-coded, and Adapted Locally - a KV-cache compression framework that uses cache states in each layer to predict keys and values in subsequent layers, then quantizes the remaining prediction residuals. We construct compact block-diagonal-plus-low-rank (block-DPLR) predictors: block-diagonal components model channel relationships within each attention head, while a global low-rank term models dependencies between heads. Coral-KV exposes a flexible fidelity-memory trade-off: it can reuse its predictors across videos or refit lightweight block-diagonal predictors to the current video for higher fidelity. Across extensive evaluations on both HY-WorldPlay and MAGI-1, our reusable configuration improves PSNR over QVG-4 by 0.32 dB and 4.95 dB, respectively, while using 48% and 37% less cache memory. Against QVG-Pro, our video-adapted configuration improves MAGI-1 PSNR by 3.77 dB while using 33.7% less cache memory. Coral-KV also maintains higher reconstruction fidelity over extended generation and reduces temporal discontinuities while retaining comparable generation latency.

Abstract: Interleaved multimodal in-context learning (ICL) lets frozen large vision-language models (LVLMs) acquire new tasks from a handful of image-text-answer demonstrations, yet it remains fragile: demonstrations bind to the wrong images, redundant exemplars crowd out informative ones, and noisy answers are copied verbatim into predictions. We present AVA, a training-free framework that organizes the demonstration pipeline of multimodal ICL into three composable stages. A Thread-Resolved Decomposer (TRD) segments the interleaved context into per-sample threads and re-stitches them with explicit boundaries, restoring image-text binding. A Weak-to-Strong Consistency Calibration (WSC) module scores each demonstration by a weak sibling model's reconstruction confidence and softly reweights its contribution during decoding. Abstract: When you ask a vision-language model (VLM) a question with an image, it answers your question based on the image. But to act means to choose: saying one thing means choosing to respond not say another. This becomes an issue when the image contains multiple features that equally relate to the question at hand: how would VLMs choose to respond? This problem is precisely studied by the classical Stroop task in psychology: among a set of deliberately designed images whose text and color conflicts (e.g., \textcolor{blue}{RED}), participants are instructed to report the color. We adapt this psychological paradigm to a benchmark and prompted a range of contrastive and generative VLMs on their behavior. Across models, we observe a persistent bias toward the feature of text in Stroop images: when text and color disagree, models preferentially generate responses centered to the text rather than color. We then opened the black box and examined the source of this bias and why certain features influences the model's behavior more than others. In the embedding space, we found that textual cues are represented more strongly and separably than color cues. This representational asymmetry predicts intervention success: steering models toward text-related directions is substantially easier than steering them toward color-related directions. Using a classical psychological paradigm, we demonstrate, at both behavioral and representational levels, that distinct features within the same image can exert unequal influence on VLM behavior. Our findings suggest that VLMs assign different degrees of saliency to different features within the same image, highlighting the need to understand how cue-specific saliency shapes their performance in widely used downstream applications.
